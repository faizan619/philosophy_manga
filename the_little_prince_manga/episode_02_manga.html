The Number and the FriendChapter IV

So I learned a second important thing. The planet he came from was hardly bigger than a house.

Besides the great planets with names, there are hundreds of others, some too small for a telescope. An astronomer gives each one a number for a name.

I believe his was Asteroid B 612. It has been seen just once, in 1909, by a Turkish astronomer.

He announced it to a great congress. Nobody believed him, because of the clothes he wore.

Then a dictator made his people dress like Europeans, or die. In 1920 he spoke again, in a smart suit. Everyone believed him.

What matters about a new friend:

What does his voice sound like?
Which games does he love?
Does he collect butterflies?

What grown-ups ask:

How old is he? How many brothers?
What does he weigh?
What does his father earn?
I saw a lovely house of pink brick, with geraniums in the windows and doves on the roof.

They can't picture it.

I saw a house worth a hundred thousand francs.
Oh, how pretty!

I'd have liked to begin as fairy tales do. Once there was a little prince whose planet was barely larger than he was, and who needed a friend.

It's six years since my friend left with his sheep. I bought a box of paints. I draw him so that I won't forget him, for it's sad to forget a friend.

He never explained anything. Perhaps he thought I was like him. But I can't see sheep through boxes. I may be a little like the grown-ups. I must have grown old.

Watch Out for the BaobabsChapter V

Sheep do eat bushes, don't they?
Yes, they do.
Oh, good!
Then they eat baobabs too?
Baobabs aren't bushes. They're trees as big as churches. A whole herd of elephants couldn't finish one.
You'd have to pile them on top of one another!
But before they grow big, baobabs start out small.

Every planet has good seeds and bad seeds. You can't see them. They sleep in the secret dark until one takes a fancy to wake.

It stretches and, shyly at first, sends up a charming, harmless little shoot toward the sun.

When they are young, a rosebush and a baobab look very much alike.

The soil of his planet was full of baobab seeds. Leave one too late and you can never get rid of it. It bores through the planet with its roots. Too many, on too small a planet, can burst it apart.

It's a question of discipline. When I've finished my own morning wash, I do the planet's.

He pulls every baobab shoot the moment he can tell it from a rose.

It's boring work, but very easy. Some jobs can wait. With baobabs, waiting is always a disaster.
I knew a planet where a lazy man lived. He neglected three little bushes…

So I drew that planet, following his instructions. I don't like to preach. But for once I'll say it outright:

Children! Watch out for the baobabs!

You may ask why no other picture in the book is as grand as this one. I tried with the others. But when I drew the baobabs, I felt it was urgent.

Forty-Three SunsetsChapter VI

Ah, little prince. Bit by bit I came to understand your sad little life. For a long time, the sweetness of sunsets was all you had to amuse you.

I'm very fond of sunsets. Come, let's go and look at one.
But we have to wait.
Wait for what?
For the sun to set.

You looked surprised. Then you laughed at yourself.

I keep thinking I'm at home!

On your tiny planet you only had to move your chair a few steps. You could see the day end whenever you wished.

One day, I saw the sun set forty-three times.
You know… when a person is very sad, they love sunsets.
The day of the forty-three… were you so very sad?

End of Episode 2

What is it really saying?

Before opening these, decide for yourself what each chapter was telling you. Then compare. These are readings, and you're free to disagree with them.

Chapter IV: numbers and clothes

We often trust the label more than the thing: the price more than the house, the suit more than the truth.

Where you see it
The astronomer in robes, then the same man in a suit. His discovery hadn't changed. Also the price tag. (Losing his colour along with his robes is this manga's choice.)
What the author says
Grown-ups like numbers and never ask about what matters. He isn't cruel about it: children must be very patient with grown-ups.
Interpretation
A number lets you feel that you know someone without meeting them. The truest line comes late: he needed a friend. No number could say that.
A fair objection
Numbers aren't the enemy. Salary, rent and age decide real things, and without numbers the astronomer couldn't report a planet at all. The mistake is stopping there. Notice too that the narrator generalises about all grown-ups, which is its own kind of label.
Easy to miss
He admits he can't see through boxes either. He stops being the judge and counts himself among the grown-ups.
Ask yourself: whom have you judged by a number: salary, marks, age, company? And which number do you use to judge yourself?
Chapter V: the baobabs

The things that ruin us start small and harmless-looking. The time to pull them out is when it's easy.

Where you see it
The two shoots that look alike, the morning wash, and the lazy man's planet.
What the author says
It's a question of discipline: boring work, but easy. Some jobs can be put off safely. This one can't.
Interpretation
On a personal level: habits, resentment, debt, a lie, a task you avoid. Daily care beats rare heroics. The author says the picture was drawn with urgency and that his friends have faced this danger for a long time without knowing it. He wrote in exile during the Second World War, so many readers see the baobabs as Nazism and fascism, small at first and ignored too long. The chapter itself never names this.
A fair objection
The two shoots look alike. Pull too early and you may kill a rose; live in constant suspicion and you may never grow anything. He waits until he can tell them apart. So the skill is careful attention, and not only discipline.
Ask yourself: what is one small baobab in your life now that would take ten minutes today and might take a year later?
Chapter VI: forty-three sunsets

Some sadness doesn't want to be solved. It wants something beautiful to look at, and someone beside it.

Where you see it
The forty-three small suns and the answer that never comes.
What the author says
When you're very sad, you love sunsets. Asked whether he was that sad, the prince doesn't reply.
Interpretation
His silence is the answer. The chapter is tiny and speaks to the prince as "you", like a letter to a friend who is gone. It also shows the pilot changing: he has started asking about a feeling, the kind of question he said grown-ups never ask.
Another reading
You could also see avoidance. Moving your chair again and again keeps you inside the same sad moment forty-three times. Beauty can comfort, and it can also be a place to hide. We don't know yet why he was sad.
Ask yourself: what do you turn to when you're sad, and does it help you pass through the feeling or stay in it?
The thread through these three chapters

Attention is a form of care: to a person behind the label, to a shoot before it's a tree, to a friend's silence.

Background, not from the story
Turkey's real law on European-style hats came in 1925, under Atatürk, so the book's dates are invented. And versions of the book differ on the number of sunsets. Chapter VI of your edition says forty-three; some say forty-four.
