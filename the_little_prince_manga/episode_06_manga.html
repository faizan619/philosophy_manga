The Seventh PlanetChapter XVI

So the seventh planet was the Earth. And the Earth is no ordinary planet.

It has all the people he'd already met, only more of them. Altogether, about two billion grown-ups.

To give you an idea of its size: before electricity, it needed an army of 462,511 lamplighters.

Seen from a distance, it was splendid. They moved like dancers in a ballet.

First New Zealand and Australia, who lit their lamps and went to bed. Then the next came on, and the next. Nobody ever missed an entrance.

Only two led an easy life: the one at the North Pole, and the one at the South. They worked twice a year.

The SnakeChapter XVII

When you try to be witty, you may fib a little. I wasn't quite honest about the lamplighters. I risk giving a false idea of our planet.

People take up very little room. Packed close together, all of humanity would fit on the smallest Pacific island.

Grown-ups won't believe you. They imagine they take up a great deal of space. They think they're as important as baobabs. Tell them to do the sum. But don't waste your own time on it.

So when he reached the Earth, the prince was surprised to see nobody at all. He was afraid he'd come to the wrong planet.

Then a ring the colour of the moon stirred in the sand.

Good evening.
Good evening.
Which planet have I come to?
Earth. In Africa.
Are there no people on Earth, then?
This is the desert. There's nobody in the desert. The Earth is large.

"I wonder," he said, "if the stars are lit so that one day each of us can find our own again. Look at my planet. It's right above us. But how far away it is!"

It's beautiful. What brings you here?
I'm having trouble with a flower.
Ah.
Where are the people? It's a little lonely in the desert.
It's lonely among people too.
You're a funny creature. As thin as a finger.
But more powerful than a king's finger.
Not very powerful. You haven't even legs. You can't go far.
I can take you farther than any ship.
Whoever I touch, I send back to the earth they came from. But you are pure, and you come from a star…
You're so weak, on this Earth made of granite. If one day you miss your planet too much, I can help you. I can…
Oh, I understood very well. But why do you always speak in riddles?
I solve them all.

A Flower with Three PetalsChapter XVIII

The prince crossed the desert and met only a flower. A flower with three petals, a nothing of a flower.

Good morning.
Good morning.
Where are the people?

She had once seen a caravan go by.

People? There are six or seven, I think. I saw them years ago. But you never know where to find them. The wind blows them about. They have no roots. It makes life hard for them.
Goodbye.
Goodbye.

The EchoChapter XIX

The only mountains he'd known were three volcanoes, as high as his knees. He used the extinct one as a stool.

"From a mountain as high as this," he thought, "I'll see the whole planet and all its people at one glance."

But he saw only needles of rock.

Hello!
Who are you?

Who are you… who are you… who are you…

Be my friends. I'm lonely.

I'm lonely… I'm lonely… I'm lonely…

"What a strange planet! Dry, and sharp, and salty. And the people have no imagination. They repeat whatever you say. At home I had a flower. She always spoke first."

Five Thousand RosesChapter XX

He walked a long way through sand, and rocks, and snow.

At last he found a road. And all roads lead to people.

Hello.
Hello.

It was a garden full of roses. He stared. They all looked like his flower.

Who are you?
We are roses.
Oh!

And he felt very unhappy. She had told him she was the only one of her kind in all the universe.

And here were five thousand, all alike, in one garden.

"She'd be so upset if she saw this. She'd cough dreadfully, and pretend to die, to escape being laughed at."

"And I'd have to pretend to nurse her. If I didn't, she'd really let herself die, to shame me too."

"I thought I was rich, with a flower like no other. I only own an ordinary rose. That, and three volcanoes up to my knees, one perhaps extinct for ever."

That doesn't make me a very great prince.

And, lying in the grass, he cried.

What is it really saying?

Before opening these, decide for yourself what each chapter was telling you. Then compare. These are readings, and you're free to disagree with them.

Chapter XVI: the Earth in numbers

Numbers can make a world look huge and tell you nothing about anyone in it.

Where you see it
The census of people he has already met.
What the author says
Earth has kings, geographers, businessmen, drunkards and swanks by the million, and before electricity an army of lamplighters moving like a ballet.
Interpretation
It's a joke at our expense: Earth is the six planets again, multiplied. The only group made beautiful is the lamplighters, the ones who served something. Work shared by many strangers, each taking a turn, becomes a dance.
Easy to miss
The numbers he lists add up to about 320 million. He says they come to about two billion. Nobody checks, because big numbers sound convincing. That may be part of the joke, and it's the same habit Chapter IV laughed at.
A fair objection
Sorting people into five silly types is unfair to the rest: parents, farmers, nurses, children. He knows it too. The next chapter begins by admitting he wasn't quite honest.
Ask yourself: when did a big number last convince you of something you didn't check?
Chapter XVII: the snake

Loneliness isn't about how many people are near. You can be alone in a desert or in a crowd.

Where you see it
"It's lonely among people too", and the stars lit so that each of us can find our own.
What the author says
People take up little room and think they're baobabs. The prince meets a snake who says it can send whoever it touches back to the earth, and offers to help him if he ever misses his planet too much.
Interpretation
The snake is speaking about death, in riddles, and the prince says he understands. It calls itself more powerful than a king, and in that sense it is. It tells the truth, gently, and it is still dangerous.
A fair objection
Wise-sounding isn't the same as right. The snake meets a lonely child soon after he lands and offers him a way out. He doesn't need a way out yet. He needs a friend. A dark sentence spoken calmly can sound like wisdom when you're sad, and that's when it most needs questioning.
A note
If this chapter feels close to how you are, please talk to someone you trust. That is also what the prince is looking for.
Ask yourself: where have you felt loneliest: by yourself, or among people? What was missing?
Chapter XVIII: the desert flower

Someone who has seen very little can still be very sure.

Where you see it
"Six or seven, I think."
What the author says
She saw one caravan, once. She concludes that there are six or seven people and that their trouble is having no roots.
Interpretation
It's comic and a little true. She judges all humanity by what passed her window. And from where she stands, moving about looks like being lost. The prince has no roots here either.
A fair objection
Rootlessness isn't only a misfortune. Roots hold you and also hold you down: she has never gone anywhere to check her opinion. For a person, roots may be people and habits more than a place.
Ask yourself: on which subject have you seen only one caravan? And what are your roots, if not a place?
Chapter XIX: the echo

If you only hear your own words coming back, you haven't met anyone yet.

Where you see it
"I'm lonely…" and "She always spoke first."
What the author says
From the mountain he sees only rock. He asks for friends and the echo answers. He concludes that people here have no imagination.
Interpretation
Two things happen. He misjudges a whole planet by talking to a cliff, as the desert flower did with a caravan. And he misses his flower for the very thing that used to tire him: she talked, she demanded, she was someone else.
A fair objection
A height gives a wide view and a poor one. He climbed so as to see everyone at one glance, and people can't be known that way. Also, we can't be too hard on him: he is very lonely, and the echo is the only voice he has.
Ask yourself: who in your life says something different from what you'd say yourself? Do you keep them close, or prefer an echo?
Chapter XX: five thousand roses

Finding out that what you love is common can break your heart. It also tells you what you were loving.

Where you see it
The garden, and "I thought I was rich".
What the author says
She told him she was the only one of her kind. Here are five thousand. He decides he owns only an ordinary rose, that he isn't a great prince, and he lies down and cries.
Interpretation
Listen to his words: rich, own, great prince. For a moment he measures his flower the way the businessman measured stars, by rarity and by what she makes him. His pride is hurt along with his love. Yet even now he is imagining how she would feel, and planning to look after her.
A fair objection
Did she lie? Perhaps she didn't know. Chapter VIII said she came as a seed and knew nothing of other places. And being hurt here isn't foolish. Most people have compared someone or something they love with a crowd of others and felt it shrink.
Leave it open
The book doesn't answer him in this chapter. Stay with the question before you read the next one.
Ask yourself: if there were five thousand just like it, what would make yours yours?
The thread through these five chapters

He has been looking for people and has met a snake, a flower, an echo and five thousand roses. Each meeting leaves him lonelier.

Look back
Everyone here judges from one narrow view: the numbers, the caravan, the mountain top, the garden. The prince does it too, about Earth and about his own flower.
Where you are
This is the lowest point of his journey so far. If you've been reading along, don't rush to Chapter XXI. Write down what you would say to him in the grass, then see what the book says.
