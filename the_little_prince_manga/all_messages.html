The book in one line

What matters most can't be seen. It's made by the time and care you give, and once you've made it, you're responsible for it.

That's one reading. Before going on, write your own line. If yours is different, keep yours and see whether the book supports it.

Five threads

1. What matters is hidden
The elephant in the snake, the sheep in the box, the child in the grown-up, the well in the desert, the rose in the sleeping boy. The book opens with a hat that isn't a hat and ends with a star that isn't just a star.
2. Time makes love
The rose isn't special because she is rare. She is special because he watered her. The fox calls it taming, and it needs patience, the same hour, a little nearer each day.
3. You answer for what you love
Baobabs must be pulled each morning, volcanoes swept, a flower sheltered. Love in this book looks like chores. The prince runs from his, learns this, and goes back.
4. People hide behind things
A crown, a hat, a bottle, a sum, a map, a hammer, four thorns. Behind each is someone lonely or afraid. The kind people in the book look behind it.
5. Growing up without going blind
The pilot is the real journey of the book. He begins as a man who hides his drawing and ends as one who draws, waits, weeps and looks up.

Argue with it

A book worth loving is worth questioning.

Children see, grown-ups don't
It's charming and unfair. The wisest figure is a fox, the kindest deed is a grown-up's, and the prince himself is often wrong: about his flower, the roses, and a whole planet he judged by an echo. A better version: anyone can see, and anyone can stop.
The heart sees better than the eyes
True about love, and risky as a rule for everything. Hearts are fooled too. The pilot needed to see a well with his heart and a broken bolt with his eyes.
Time given makes it precious
Often. It also keeps people in bad jobs and bad love. Ask too whether the thing is good for you, and whether it gives anything back.
You're responsible for ever
A beautiful promise and a heavy one. Care has to include yourself, and some ties may rightly be loosened.
Who gets to speak
Nearly everyone who thinks and travels in this story is male, and the one main female character is a vain flower who waits. We hear her only through him. It's worth imagining Chapter VIII told by the rose.
The ending
It's tender, and it makes leaving look gentle. Read it as a fable about grief, not as a guide. The pilot who stays is the example to follow.

For you

Answer these in a notebook. They're worth more than anything written above.

Your Drawing Number 1
What did you stop showing people?
Your baobab
What small thing should be pulled out this week?
Your rose
Who or what have you given time to? Do they know?
Your planet
Which of the six men is most like you on a bad day?
Your fox
Who was patient enough to tame you?
Your well
What would you walk all night to find?
And the last one is the book's own: look at the sky. Has the sheep eaten the flower, or not?

Background, not from the story: the book came out in New York in April 1943. Saint-Exupéry went back to flying for the Free French and disappeared on a reconnaissance flight on 31 July 1944. Léon Werth, the friend in the dedication, survived the war. Many readers hear the ending differently once they know this. The author couldn't have meant it as a prophecy.
