Before the storythe dedication

The author gives this book to his best friend, a grown-up named Léon Werth, who is hungry and cold in wartime France. Then he apologises to children for choosing a grown-up, and changes it. The book is for his friend when he was a little boy.

Every grown-up was a child once, he says. Only a few of them remember.

The HatChapter I

I was six when I found it: a picture in a book about the jungle. A boa had wrapped itself around a wild animal and was swallowing it.

It doesn't even chew, said the book. Afterwards it can't move. It sleeps for six months until the meal is gone.

I thought about jungles for a long time. Then I took a coloured pencil and made my first drawing.

I showed the grown-ups.

Does it scare you?
Scared? Why would anyone be scared of a hat?

It wasn't a hat. It was a boa digesting an elephant. So I drew the inside as well, because grown-ups always need things explained.

Put your snakes away. Study geography. History. Arithmetic. Grammar.

And so, at six, I gave up a splendid career as a painter.

I learned to fly instead, all over the world. Geography did turn out to be useful. I can tell China from Arizona at a glance, which helps when you're lost at night.

Whenever I met a grown-up who seemed bright, I tested them with Drawing Number 1. I had kept it all that time.

It's a hat.

So I never mentioned boas, or rainforests, or stars. I talked about bridge, golf, politics and ties. They were delighted.

Such a sensible man!

Draw Me a SheepChapter II

So I lived alone, with nobody I could really talk to. Then, six years ago, my engine broke down in the Sahara.

No mechanic. No passengers. A hard repair to do alone, and barely eight days of water. It was life or death.

The first night I slept on the sand, a thousand miles from anyone. A castaway on a raft in mid-ocean was less alone.

Please… draw me a sheep!
What?
Draw me a sheep…

I jumped up and rubbed my eyes. A most extraordinary little fellow stood there, watching me. He wasn't lost. He wasn't tired or hungry or thirsty. He wasn't afraid.

What on earth are you doing here?
Please… draw me a sheep.

When a mystery is that big, you don't dare disobey. I took out pen and paper.

I can't draw!
That doesn't matter.

I had never drawn a sheep. So I gave him one of the only two drawings I knew.

No, no! Not an elephant inside a boa! A boa is dangerous, and an elephant takes up far too much room. My home is tiny. I need a sheep.
That one's already very ill.
That's a ram. It has horns.
Too old. I want one that lives long.

I had an engine to take apart and my patience was gone. I scribbled something and tossed it at him.

That's the box. The sheep you want is inside.

And his whole face lit up.

That's exactly how I wanted it! Will he need a lot of grass?
I gave you a very small one.
Not so small as that… Look! He's fallen asleep.

And that is how I met the little prince.

Someone from the SkyChapter III

He asked me many questions and never seemed to hear mine. I learned about him by accident, from things he let slip.

What's that thing?
It isn't a thing. It flies. It's my plane.
What! You fell out of the sky?
Yes.
Oh, how funny!

His laughter annoyed me a great deal. I like my misfortunes taken seriously.

So you came from the sky too! Which planet is yours?
…too?
You're from another planet, then?

He didn't answer. He shook his head at my plane.

You can't have come very far in that.
Where is your home? Where will you take my sheep?

A long, thoughtful silence.

What's good about the box is that he can use it as a house at night.
If you're good, I'll give you a rope and a stake, to tie him up by day.
Tie him up? What a strange idea!
But he'll wander off and get lost.
Where would he go?
Anywhere. Straight ahead.
It wouldn't matter. It's so small, where I come from…
Even straight ahead, you couldn't get very far.

End of Episode 1

What is it really saying?

Before opening these, decide for yourself what each chapter was telling you. Then compare. These are readings, and you're free to disagree with them.

Before the story: who is this book for?

Growing up is not the problem. Forgetting that you were a child is.

Where you see it
The grown-up with a child's shadow. (The shadow is this manga's own image, not the book's.)
What the author says
All grown-ups were children first, though few of them remember it.
Interpretation
So "grown-up" in this book isn't really an age. The dedication even praises a grown-up who understands everything. It means a person who has stopped seeing.
Chapter I: the hat

Knowing a lot is not the same as seeing. People look at the outside, give it a name, and stop looking.

Where you see it
"Why would anyone be scared of a hat?" and the pilot in a hat and tie. (Dressing him like them is this manga's addition.)
What the author says
Grown-ups never understand anything by themselves, and children get tired of explaining. He stopped drawing, and later talked to people only about what they liked.
Interpretation
The real loss isn't the drawing. Nobody asked him "What is it?" They only told him what it was. He answered by hiding the part of himself that nobody saw, and so he ended up alone.
A fair objection
The drawing does look like a hat, and you could call the test unfair. One wrong answer and he closes the door on a person. He judges people by one look, just as they judged his drawing. And the school subjects weren't worthless: he admits geography helped him.
Ask yourself: what is your Drawing Number 1, the thing you stopped showing people? Did they refuse to see it, or did you stop explaining too soon?
Chapter II: the sheep in the box

Being understood can arrive from where you least expect it. And what you leave open, another person can fill with their imagination.

Where you see it
The prince sees the elephant at once, then his face lights up at a plain box.
What the author says
Three sheep drawings are refused. The box, scribbled in a hurry, is exactly right.
Interpretation
The prince passes the test nobody else passed, and he isn't even trying. The hat and the box also match: both hide something inside. Grown-ups saw only the outside of one. The prince sees inside both.
Another reading
The pilot wasn't wise here. He was impatient and wanted to get rid of the boy. The box works because of what the prince brings to it. You could also say the chapter is about listening more than imagination: a man facing death stops to answer a child's small request.
Ask yourself: when did someone understand you without an explanation? Have you done that for anyone?
Chapter III: straight ahead

You learn who someone is by paying attention, not by questioning them. And what looks like safety to one person looks strange to another.

Where you see it
The rope and stake, and the last sky.
What the author says
The prince ignores direct questions, and the truth comes out bit by bit through chance remarks. His final line carries perhaps a touch of sadness.
Interpretation
The pilot's first thought about something precious is to tie it down so it isn't lost. The prince's first thought is that it will have a house. But his home is so small that freedom there has little room. That may be where the sadness comes from.
A fair objection
The pilot's offer is kind and practical. Sheep do wander off. Caring for something sometimes needs a limit, and the book hasn't settled that question yet.
Ask yourself: is there something you hold tightly because you're afraid to lose it? Is the rope for its sake or for yours?
The thread through all three chapters

What matters is often inside, where a quick look can't reach: the elephant in the snake, the sheep in the box, the child in the grown-up.

This is a pattern in what you've read so far, so treat it as a working idea. Watch whether the book confirms it, changes it, or argues with it.

Background, not from the story: Saint-Exupéry was a real pilot. In December 1935 he crashed in the Libyan desert with his mechanic and nearly died of thirst. In the story the pilot is alone.
